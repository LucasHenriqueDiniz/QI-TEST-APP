# Gonthier (2023), "Should Intelligence Tests Be Speeded or Unspeeded? A Brief Review of the Effects of Time Pressure on Response Processes and an Experimental Study with Raven's Matrices", Journal of Intelligence 11(6):120, doi:10.3390/jintelligence11060120

> Fetched 2026-10-19 · Peer-reviewed review + experiment (PMC full text; MDPI returned 403). Read for the history of timing on Raven's matrices and for what a time limit does to reasoning items. Read through WebFetch's summariser over the first 100k characters; quotes are as returned.

## What it says

- John C. Raven (1938) noted that the progressive matrices "cannot be given satisfactorily with a time-limit".
- John Raven (2008), high-jump analogy: "it would not make sense to set a time limit within which people have to show how high they can jump" … "whilst also insisting that that they start by jumping over the lowest bar."
- Speeding means "shifting the focus from a pure power test to a mix of power and speed."
- Later practice: "norms were ultimately made available for different time limits" (Raven et al., 1998).
- "A meta-analysis based on Raven's matrices indicated that using a time limit substantially changes correlations between reasoning performance other constructs." (Tatel et al.; see `tatel-2022-raven-speeded-meta-analysis.md`.)
- "Time pressure may affect the response processes which translate individual differences" …
- Abstract: time pressure produced "a substantial decrease of accuracy (d = 0.35), even when controlling for response time at the item level", i.e. "a detrimental effect on cognitive processing beyond speeding"; "time pressure disproportionately reduced response times for difficult items".
- Abstract conclusion: the effects "go well beyond forcing participants to speed or skip the last few items and make even mild time constraints inadvisable when attempting to measure maximal performance, especially for high-performing samples."
- On scoring speed: some approaches use "composite scores that combine accuracy and speed", but "being fast is not necessarily a good thing," and "a low speed also reflects carefulness."
- Discussion: those who speed the test "for practical reasons may want to consider" telling participants the typical task duration.

## What it does not say

- That any published IQ battery scores per-item response time on matrices. It discusses accuracy-speed composites as a research approach, not as clinical practice.
- The second half of the article (characters 100k–216k) was not read.

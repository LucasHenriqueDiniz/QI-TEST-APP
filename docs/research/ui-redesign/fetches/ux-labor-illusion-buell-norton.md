# The Labor Illusion: How Operational Transparency Increases Perceived Value (Buell & Norton, Management Science 57(9), 1564–1579, 2011)

> Fetched 2026-10-19 · Harvard Business School faculty publication page with the paper's abstract; read to evaluate a "calculating your result" moment between the last question and the Results screen. Full text was not accessible (the HBS PDF link returned HTTP 403); the same abstract is on RePEc (https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v57y2011i9p1564-1579.html).

## What it says

Abstract (verbatim, HBS page):

> "A ubiquitous feature of even the fastest self-service technology transactions is the wait. Conventional wisdom and operations theory suggests that the longer people wait, the less satisfied they become; we demonstrate that due to what we term the labor illusion, when websites engage in operational transparency by signaling that they are exerting effort, people can actually prefer websites with longer waits to those that return instantaneous results—even when those results are identical. In five experiments that simulate service experiences in the domains of online travel and online dating, we demonstrate the impact of the labor illusion on service value perceptions, demonstrate that perceptions of service provider effort induce feelings of reciprocity that together mediate the link between operational transparency and increased valuation, and explore boundary conditions and alternative explanations."

Citation as shown: "Buell, Ryan W., and Michael I. Norton. "The Labor Illusion: How Operational Transparency Increases Perceived Value." Management Science 57, no. 9 (September 2011): 1564–1579."

Related follow-up read in the same session (Harvard DASH copy of HBS Working Paper 14-115, Buell, Kim & Tsay, "Creating Reciprocal Value Through Operational Transparency", published Management Science 2017, doi 10.1287/mnsc.2015.2411), abstract:

> "Laboratory studies revealed that customers who observed employees engaging in labor perceived greater effort, better appreciated that effort, and valued the service more."

## What it does not say

- The abstract does not state the boundary conditions it "explores" (e.g., how long the wait can be before the effect reverses, or whether it holds for unfavourable outcomes). I could not read the full paper, so these limits are unestablished here.
- The key ingredient is *transparency* — "signaling that they are exerting effort" — not the delay itself. A plain spinner is not what was tested; the travel-site manipulation showed the work being done. For the app this implies step text ("Scoring 30 answers… Comparing with the norm curve… Computing your percentile") rather than an empty wait.
- Nothing about self-administered tests, or about honesty concerns when the computation is actually instantaneous. The M3 loading-indicator guideline (`ux-m3-loading-indicator.md`) says sub-200ms work should show no indicator — a tension the redesign has to resolve deliberately.

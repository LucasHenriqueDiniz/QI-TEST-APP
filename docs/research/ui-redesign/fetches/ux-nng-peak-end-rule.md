# The Peak–End Rule: How Impressions Become Memories (NN/g, Lexie Kane, 2018-12-30)

> Fetched 2026-10-19 · NN/g summary of Kahneman & Fredrickson's peak–end rule applied to UX; read to justify investing in the Results screen (the "end") and one positive "peak", and avoiding negative peaks.

## What it says

> "Peak–end rule: A cognitive bias that impacts how people remember past events. Intense positive or negative moments (the "peaks") and the final moments of an experience (the "end") are heavily weighted in our mental calculus."

> "As it turns out, the slightly less uncomfortable final 30 seconds of the experiment changed how people perceived the entirety of Round 2. 80% of the study participants preferred Round 2 and chose to repeat that condition in the final trial."

> "A bright color, an icon, or a lively illustration at the end of a successful interaction can solidify a good experience in your users' memories. For example, Duolingo is an educational app that gamifies the process of learning a language."

> "Duolingo uses its cartoon mascot to encourage users throughout the learning process. Here the app congratulates users for answering 10 questions correctly."

> "Identify the moments when your product is most helpful, valuable, or entertaining and design to make those moments even better."

> "People remember negative experiences more vividly than positive ones. Moments of confusion and frustration also act as "peaks" in the peak–end rule"

> "Last impressions are lasting impressions. [...] Ensure that your users' experience of your product concludes on a high note."

> "The power of the last impressions in UI design is well illustrated by progress indicators: the HCI literature shows that, if a progress indicator speeds up towards the end, the process is judged to have been faster than if the speed of the progress indicator was constant."

> "In TurboTax, the arguably complex flow of filing income taxes ends with a screen that celebrates the end of the process, acknowledging and enhancing most users' sense of relief."

> "The application embraces the natural end point of the experience. It does not attempt to overstay its welcome in users' lives through content hooks or attempts to upsell other products."

> "We caution designers against using "please-don't-go" pop-ups that that beg users not to leave a site. These interface elements irritate users and contribute to a negative impression of the site later on."

References listed on the page: Harrison, Amento, Kuznetsov, Bell (2007) "Rethinking the Progress Bar", UIST; Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber, Redelmeier (1993) "When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End", Psychological Science.

## What it does not say

- Nothing about how to handle a *disappointing* end (a low score) — the rule implies the result screen can be a negative peak, but the article gives no pattern for softening bad news.
- Note for the redesign: an interstitial/ad placed right before or on the results screen sits exactly on the "end"; the article doesn't discuss ads but its "please-don't-go" caution is the closest analogue.

# The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected: Purchase Acceleration, Illusionary Goal Progress, and Customer Retention (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, Journal of Marketing Research 43(1), 39–58, 2006)

> Fetched 2026-10-19 · The original field-experiment paper behind the "goal-gradient effect" (the source Laws of UX cites); read for how proximity to a goal and pre-credited progress affect effort — relevant to progress display and milestones in a 30-question test. Pages 1–2 (abstract + summary of findings) read from the Columbia Business School copy.

## What it says

Abstract (verbatim):

> "The goal-gradient hypothesis denotes the classic finding from behaviorism that animals expend more effort as they approach a reward. Building on this hypothesis, the authors generate new propositions for the human psychology of rewards. They test these propositions using field experiments, secondary customer data, paper-and-pencil problems, and Tobit and logit models. The key findings indicate that (1) participants in a real café reward program purchase coffee more frequently the closer they are to earning a free coffee; (2) Internet users who rate songs in return for reward certificates visit the rating Web site more often, rate more songs per visit, and persist longer in the rating effort as they approach the reward goal; (3) the illusion of progress toward the goal induces purchase acceleration (e.g., customers who receive a 12-stamp coffee card with 2 preexisting "bonus" stamps complete the 10 required purchases faster than customers who receive a "regular" 10-stamp card); and (4) a stronger tendency to accelerate toward the goal predicts greater retention and faster reengagement in the program. The conceptualization and empirical findings are captured by a parsimonious goal-distance model, in which effort investment is a function of the proportion of original distance remaining to the goal."

From the introduction's summary of findings (p. 40):

> "Members of a café RP (e.g., "buy ten coffees, get one free") purchase coffee more frequently the closer they are to earning a free coffee (on average, interpurchase times decrease by 20% or .7 days throughout the program)."

> "We capture all of the findings with a parsimonious goal-distance model (GDM), in which effort investment is a function of the proportion of original distance remaining to the goal (i.e., psychological distance)."

> "Notably, purchase and effort rates reset (to a lower level) after the first reward is earned and then reaccelerate toward the second reward goal."

## What it does not say

- It studies loyalty programs and song rating, not questionnaires or tests; transfer to "question 22 of 30" is an inference.
- It does not test segmented progress bars. The "reset after the first reward" finding cuts both ways for milestones every 10 questions: each sub-goal creates a new near-goal acceleration, but motivation drops right after each milestone is reached.
- "Illusionary" pre-credited progress (the 2 bonus stamps) is not directly portable to an IQ test without misrepresenting progress (compare the ethics caution in `ux-survey-progress-meta-analysis.md`). An honest analogue would be counting an intro/practice item as step 1, which the paper does not test.

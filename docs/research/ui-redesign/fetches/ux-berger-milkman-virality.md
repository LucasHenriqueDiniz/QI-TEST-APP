# What Makes Online Content Viral? (Berger & Milkman, Journal of Marketing Research 49(2), 192–205, 2012)

> Fetched 2026-10-19 · The original JMR paper (Wharton-hosted pre-print, "Ahead of Print" version) on which emotions drive sharing; read to decide the emotional framing and self-presentation value of the result share card. Pages 1–2 read.

## What it says

Abstract (verbatim):

> "Why are certain pieces of online content (e.g., advertisements, videos, news articles) more viral than others? This article takes a psychological approach to understanding diffusion. Using a unique data set of all the New York Times articles published over a three-month period, the authors examine how emotion shapes virality. The results indicate that positive content is more viral than negative content, but the relationship between emotion and social transmission is more complex than valence alone. Virality is partially driven by physiological arousal. Content that evokes high-arousal positive (awe) or negative (anger or anxiety) emotions is more viral. Content that evokes low-arousal, or deactivating, emotions (e.g., sadness) is less viral. These results hold even when the authors control for how surprising, interesting, or practically useful content is (all of which are positively linked to virality), as well as external drivers of attention (e.g., how prominently content was featured). Experimental results further demonstrate the causal impact of specific emotion on transmission and illustrate that it is driven by the level of activation induced."

Self-presentation motive (p. 1–2):

> "Consumers may share such practically useful content for altruistic reasons (e.g., to help others) or for self-enhancement purposes (e.g., to appear knowledgeable, see Wojnicki and Godes 2008)."

> "Consumers often share content for self-presentation purposes (Wojnicki and Godes 2008) or to communicate identity, and consequently, positive content may be shared more because it reflects positively on the sender. Most people would prefer to be known as someone who shares upbeat stories or makes others feel good rather than someone who shares things that makes others sad or upset."

> "People report discussing many of their emotional experiences with others, and customers report greater word of mouth at the extremes of satisfaction (i.e., highly satisfied or highly dissatisfied; Anderson 1998)."

## What it does not say

- Studies news articles, not personal result cards; transfer to "share my IQ score" is an inference.
- Implication drawn for the app (not stated by the paper): sharing is most likely when the card makes the sender look good (high scores, a flattering strength label) and evokes high-arousal positive emotion; low or middling scores are a low-share case, so the card should offer something sender-flattering for every score (e.g., top category strength) — this is design inference.
- Nothing on story format, image size, or platform (WhatsApp/Instagram).
